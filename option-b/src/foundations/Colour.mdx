# Colour

Colour here is two layers, and keeping them apart is the entire point.

A **primitive** is a value with no meaning. `n-600` is a near-grey, nothing more. There are
{Object.keys(palette).length} of them, they are generated from an OKLCH design intent and verified
in the sRGB gamut, and no component may reference one directly.

A **role** is a decision. `content-muted` says "this text is secondary but still meant to be read",
and it happens to resolve to `n-600` today. There are {Object.keys(lightColors).length} roles, and
they are the only names a component is allowed to use, through `t('content-muted')`.

The reason for the split is regression, not tidiness. The app this replaces had 67 hardcoded colour
literals and no role layer, so every theme change was a search and replace, and every search and
replace missed something. With a role layer, a theme is a set of role definitions and a component
does not change at all.

**Dark mode only ever redefines roles.** Primitives never move. Both maps are keyed identically
({Object.keys(darkColors).length} roles each), which is what makes "the same product with the lamp
off" a checkable claim rather than a hope. Dark is not inverted light: each dark surface is built at
the same chroma as its light counterpart and at matched contrast steps, because inverting lightness
alone produces a dark theme that is grey where the light theme is tinted, and the two then stop
looking like one product.

## The inversion this option makes

Most product UI puts the grey on the page and the white on the card, so depth runs toward the frame.
This one does the opposite, and it is the single decision the whole layout rests on.

The **rail** is the darkest chrome. The **list column** is tinted. The **detail pane**, where the
agent's write-up is, is the brightest surface on screen. Depth runs toward the content: the eye is
pulled to the thing you are reading rather than to the furniture around it, and the queue you are
choosing from is legibly the supporting cast.

<SurfaceLadder />

That ordering survives into dark mode. `surface-nav` is the deepest step, `surface-sunken` sits above
it, and `surface-default` is still the lightest of the three. A dark theme that quietly flipped the
ladder would put the brightest thing on screen behind the navigation, which is exactly the mistake
this design was built to avoid.

Two consequences worth naming. The bright pane needs almost no border to read as a plane, which is
why this system has fewer border roles than a card-on-grey layout would need. And the canvas
(`surface-canvas`) is only ever the hairline gap between panes, so it is the one surface a reader
never actually looks at.

## Primitives

Grouped by ramp. The neutral ramp does most of the work, and it is very nearly a grey: every step
carries a trace of the accent hue, but only a trace, at half the chroma it was first built with.
That halving is the 08-21 correction. The first pass tinted the neutrals at the same chroma as the
accent, and the verdict on it was fair: the room read purple, rather than reading as a grey room
with a plum accent in it. The hue now lives in the accent, and the neutrals only carry enough of it
to keep tinted chrome from looking dirty beside a white pane.

The **accent ramp desaturates at both ends**, which is worth knowing before reading a swatch as a
mistake. `a-50` and `a-900` are not decoration, they are large tinted planes: the selected row, the
suggested-fix panel, the active-filter chips. Held at the chroma of `a-500` they read as a state
rather than as a surface. So the middle of the ramp is fully plum and the ends are nearly graphite,
and the rule that falls out of it is the one this option now follows everywhere: **the accent at
full strength only ever appears on a small shape.**

### Actions

Primary **is** the accent here, which is the whole point of a committed palette: the plum carries
selection and the primary action, so a reader learns one colour and it means "this is the thing to
act on". In dark mode it steps one shade lighter, because a mid-plum on near-black glows rather than
reads. The brand mark is **not** part of this, and has its own role below.

### The brand, which is not an accent

One role, one element: the Melonade mark in the rail. It is watermelon, taken off the live landing
page, and it is the only watermelon thing in the app.

It is separate from the accent for a reason that is not taste. Watermelon sits about 24 degrees from
this option's danger ramp in OKLCH, so an accent at that hue would put the selected row, the
suggested-fix panel and a red alarm in the same family, and the impact signal would stop meaning
anything. Plum sits 68 degrees off danger, which is the whole reason this option can afford a
committed accent at all.

So the hue that means *Melonade* and the hue that means *act on this* are deliberately two different
hues, and the split is enforced by naming rather than by discipline: `brand-mark` is not wired into
any semantic role, and if it ever appears on a control that is a bug. A logo is identity. An accent
is a signal. Only one of them is allowed to be everywhere.
