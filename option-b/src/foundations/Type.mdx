# Type

## The root stays at 16px

The root font size is deliberately the browser's untouched default. The app this replaces sets it to
14px, which quietly rescales every `rem` in the codebase: a component that says `0.875rem` renders at
12.25px there and 14px anywhere else, which is why type beside a framework control never lined up.
Worse, moving the root moves it out from under the reader's own zoom and accessibility settings, so a
person who has set a larger default gets a smaller app.

Density comes from the scale instead, and this option chooses a 14px base where the denser option
chooses 13px. That is the trade being made here in one number: fewer rows on screen, and a queue you
can read at a glance rather than scan.

## Sizes

Nine steps, ratio around 1.14. The bottom five are the interface, the top two are the serif, and the
two in the middle exist so a pane heading does not have to borrow a display size.

## Weights

Three, and a fourth is how a quiet system starts shouting. Weight carries hierarchy inside a row
where size cannot: a list row goes from regular to medium when it becomes the selected issue, and the
row height does not move.

## Line height

Looser than a dense table would use. The detail pane is prose that people actually read, so
`leading-relaxed` is a real working value here rather than a token kept for completeness.

## Tracking

Negative tracking at the reading sizes, because the sans is slightly condensed. `tracking-wide` is
for the uppercase micro-labels only, where the caps need the air back, and `tracking-serif` exists
because a high-contrast serif at 26px sets loose and needs pulling in.

## The serif

The pairing is on a **contrast axis**, not on similarity. Instrument Sans is a slightly condensed
grotesk; Instrument Serif is a high-contrast display serif. Two grotesks that are almost the same is
the pairing mistake nobody can see, and sans against serif is a decision anyone can.

<SerifVsSans />

### It is display only, and that is enforced by a class

`.m-display` is the only way the serif enters the app. It is a class rather than a heading rule
precisely so that adding a heading somewhere cannot accidentally put a display face on a label.

It appears in exactly **two** places:

- the **issue title** at the top of the detail pane, at `text-3xl`
- the **empty-state headline**, at `text-4xl`

It never touches a label, a button, a control, a chip, a table cell or a piece of metadata. The
reason is not taste, it is what a display face does at small sizes in interface chrome: the contrast
that makes it good at 32px makes it thin and slow to read at 13px, and a work tool set in a display
serif reads as costumed rather than serious. The left column below is every legitimate use in the
product. The right column is the failure this rule exists to prevent, and it is worth looking at,
because it is what "just use the nice font for the section headers too" actually looks like.

<SerifPlacement />

The italic is loaded and unused. That is deliberate: one voice for the write-up title, and no second
emphasis mechanism competing with weight.
