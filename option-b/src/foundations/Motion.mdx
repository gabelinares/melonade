# Motion

Four durations and two easings, and the numbers are small on purpose. This is a work tool: the
animation's job is to explain that something moved, then get out of the way. Nothing bounces, nothing
is elastic, and nothing waits for the reader.

## On the two things that actually animate

The product has almost no motion in it, and that is a decision rather than an omission. A triage
console is read, not toured. So the durations below are shown first on the two places motion genuinely
exists: a row taking its hover surface, and a control answering a press. Hover the row, then press and
hold the button.

## Durations

Hover the box. All four tokens run the same distance at the same moment, which is the only way the
60ms between `duration-fast` and `duration-normal` is perceptible at all. Read them as a scale of
distance rather than of importance: `duration-instant` is a colour change on a row, `duration-fast` is
a chevron rotating or a control pressing, `duration-normal` is a popover arriving, and `duration-slow`
is reserved for something crossing the screen, which in this layout is a drawer and nothing else.

## Easings

Press the button. Both run at `duration-slow` so the curves have room to differ.

`ease-out` here is an ease-out quint, which has a longer tail than the usual quart: it leaves
immediately and settles slowly, and that tail is what makes a pane change feel unhurried rather than
snappy. It is right for anything the reader started, because the interface should feel like it
responded before the animation finished. `ease-in-out` is symmetrical and is for something moving from
one place to another under its own steam rather than in reply to a click.

## The reduced-motion contract

`prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` is honoured once, globally, in the base layer: every animation and
transition collapses to 0.01ms, iteration counts drop to 1, and smooth scrolling is turned off.

Two consequences worth stating. A component must never be the thing that declares its own
reduced-motion handling, because a global rule that components can opt out of is not a contract. And
no state may be communicated by motion alone: every transition here is a change of colour, position or
size that is equally readable in its final frame, so a reader with motion turned off loses the
transition and nothing else. The selected row is the test case, and it passes because selection is a
surface and a weight, not a slide.
