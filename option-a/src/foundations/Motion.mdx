# Motion

Four durations and two easings, and the numbers are small on purpose. This is a work tool: the
animation's job is to explain that something moved, then get out of the way. Nothing bounces, nothing
is elastic, and nothing waits for the reader.

## Durations

Hover the box. All four tokens run the same distance at the same moment, which is the only way the
50ms between `duration-fast` and `duration-normal` is perceptible at all. Read them as a scale of
distance rather than of importance: `duration-instant` is a colour change on a nav row,
`duration-fast` is a chevron rotating, `duration-normal` is the nav collapsing to a rail, and
`duration-slow` is reserved for something crossing the screen.

## Easings

Press the button. Both run at `duration-slow` so the curves have room to differ.

`ease-out` is an ease-out quart: it leaves immediately and settles softly, which is right for
anything the reader started, because the interface should feel like it responded before the animation
finished. `ease-in-out` is symmetrical and is for something moving from one place to another under its
own steam rather than in reply to a click.

## The reduced-motion contract

`prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` is honoured once, globally, in the base layer: every animation and
transition collapses to 0.01ms, iteration counts drop to 1, and smooth scrolling is turned off. The
skeleton shimmer is handled separately, as a flat surface fill rather than a stopped gradient, because
an animation frozen mid-sweep leaves a visible stripe.

Two consequences worth stating. A component must never be the thing that declares its own
reduced-motion handling, because a global rule that components can opt out of is not a contract. And
no state may be communicated by motion alone: every transition here is a change of colour, position
or size that is equally readable in its final frame, so a reader with motion turned off loses the
transition and nothing else.
